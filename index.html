I am a second-year law student at WashU School of Law in St. Louis, where I am a Scholar in Law Merit Scholar, Dean’s Research Fellow, AI in Law Fellow, and staff editor of the Washington University Jurisprudence Review, the only student-edited, in-print journal of jurisprudence in the United States.

I am from the small, rural town of Winnsboro, South Carolina, but chose to go one state over to complete my undergraduate education at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, where I served as the 100th student body president. I earned a master’s degree in government and policy from Johns Hopkins University.

Before law school I spent several years in housing and community development in Washington, D.C., working at a high-impact, national nonprofit organization focused on community development policy. There I directed advocacy and organizing for more than 180 community development organizations and worked with D.C. agencies and elected officials to design and implement solutions in community development.

During law school, I’ve had the pleasure of working with, and learning from, incredible legal minds, including the Hon. Michael McNamara (18th District Court of Michigan), the Hon. Jonathan J.C. Grey (Eastern District of Michigan), and the Hon. Algenon L. Marbley (Southern District of Ohio). I’ve also worked at several high-impact litigation firms and organizations, including Dickinson Wright PLLC, the Williams Institute at UCLA, the Michigan State Appellate Defender Office, and the Center for Death Penalty Litigation.

My legal scholarship treats homelessness as a constitutional problem. I write about the right to shelter, about what choice and consent mean when a person has no real options, about how zoning decides who gets to live and vote in a community, and about the state of public education.

My role

I created Community Voices Academy and designed its curriculum while I was at the Coalition for Nonprofit Housing and Economic Development in Washington, D.C. I built it so that residents who have experienced homelessness have the skills and the access to influence the policies that affect them.

Since 2022 the Academy has graduated three cohorts. Its 50 alumni are working across D.C., and a quarter of them now hold elected or appointed positions.

Sessions are hands-on and run about an hour. Participants work through short debates, sorting exercises, whiteboard brainstorming, stakeholder mapping, and message testing with one another, so every lesson ends with something they can use.

Student Note · Washington University Jurisprudence Review · the shelter

Choice?

What does it mean to say yes when every other door has been locked? Many shelters require sobriety, a curfew, religious programming, or giving up a pet before a person can have a bed, and after City of Grants Pass v. Johnson (2024) the only alternative, sleeping outside, can lead to a citation, a fine, and eventually jail.

Providers, agencies, and courts still treat that yes as consent. Drawing on the jurisprudence of consent, unconscionability, and the unconstitutional conditions doctrine, this Note argues that when the state has foreclosed every functional alternative, the law should stop mistaking a person’s last remaining option for a choice.

Law review article · the road

Shaping an Electorate

Long before anyone casts a ballot in a city, a zoning map has already decided who gets to live there. Debates over malls, housing supply, and data centers rarely ask what land use does to political power, yet the rules that control density and housing stock can decide which communities ever become voters in a municipality at all.

This Article traces the history of racial residential exclusion behind those rules and proposes that states require preclearance of certain land use decisions under a three-step disparate impact test: whether the decision significantly limits a protected class’s ability to reside, whether a substantial interest requires it, and whether a less discriminatory alternative exists.

Dean’s Research Fellowship · with Professor Kimberly Norwood · the school

The Apocalypse is Now.

In the oldest sense of the word, an apocalypse is an unveiling, and the dismantling of American public education is one by design. Under the banners of “merit” and “fiscal discipline,” charter schools take public money without public obligations, vouchers and education savings accounts route tax dollars to private schools, and public universities are losing Black students, Graduate PLUS loans, and their independence from political control.

This Article shows that these measures accelerated once public school enrollment became majority non-white, and it looks to Chile, which turned its schools into a voucher market in 1981 and is still paying for it forty-five years later, to show where this road leads.

Law review article · the house

Status Recognition and the Right to Shelter

Advocates have called shelter a human right for decades, and American courts have refused to say so for just as long. City of Grants Pass v. Johnson (2024) exposed how little ground anti-criminalization doctrine had ever secured.

This Article finds a way forward in the history of the right to counsel, which began when the Supreme Court recognized indigence as an involuntary condition and grew into the affirmative obligation of Gideon v. Wainwright. Once homelessness is recognized as an involuntary status on those same terms, the Gideon framework supports a constitutional obligation to give every person a non-criminal alternative to sleeping outside.

Presented at the American Bar Association Forum on Affordable Housing and Community Development, and at Fordham Law, 2026

Law review article · Williams Institute, UCLA School of Law

Anticipatory Reliance and the Illusion of Rights Security for Marriage Equality

How secure is a constitutional right once millions of people have built their families, homes, and finances around it, and the Court signals it may take it back? Using marriage equality as its case study, this Article develops a theory of anticipatory reliance to measure what people stand to lose when the security of a recognized right turns out to be an illusion.

Developed during my Spring and Summer 2026 fellowship at the Williams Institute, where I also researched public records law on access to administrative data in more than ten states.

Emerging Legal Scholar

Lamar Gregory Richards

My research examines the legal remedies that can be used to end homelessness.

How can we bridge the gap between social science, social policy, and constitutional law so that the millions of people who are unhoused or insecurely housed have rights that courts will recognize and enforce? My work looks at fundamental constitutional rights through the lens of the unhoused, with each piece moving closer to a legal framework that can survive judicial scrutiny and make the case for a human right to shelter.
